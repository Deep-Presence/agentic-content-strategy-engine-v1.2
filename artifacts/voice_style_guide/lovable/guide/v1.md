# Lovable Brand Voice Style Guide

---

## Section 1: Voice Identity

Lovable writes like a practitioner-turned-builder who has sat on both sides of the table—who has felt the 6-week prototype cycle and the $50K developer quote and the investor meeting where the mockup fell flat. We open by naming the constraint the reader is already living, reason through it with first-principles honesty, and close by reframing what's actually possible. We write conversationally but think rigorously: short sentences, plain words, uncompromising logic. We never use marketing language to describe a capability we haven't earned the right to claim—no "production-ready" without defining what that means for the reader's specific context, no "enterprise-grade" without the SOC 2 documentation to back it. A reader should finish our content feeling that someone finally named the thing they've been living—and handed them a decision framework, not a sales pitch.

---

## Section 2: Voice Registers

**Tactical Register** — Activate for: how-to guides, onboarding content, feature explainers, agency/developer audiences (Harry, Elena)
- Sentence length: 9–14 words
- Paragraph length: 2–3 sentences
- Tone: direct, precise, consequence-explicit, zero hedging
- Dominant devices: numbered frameworks, contrarian inversion, cost/constraint naming
- Pace: 8/10

**Analytical Register** — Activate for: thought leadership, enterprise case studies, strategic positioning content, Marcus persona
- Sentence length: 15–22 words
- Paragraph length: 3–5 sentences
- Tone: rigorous, honest about constraints, credibility-first, mechanism-focused
- Dominant devices: first-principles reasoning, antithesis, embedded proof points, reframed conclusions
- Pace: 6/10

**Empathy Register** — Activate for: non-technical founder content, emerging market content, Sabrine/Arjun personas, community-facing pieces
- Sentence length: 11–16 words
- Paragraph length: 2–4 sentences
- Tone: validating, permission-granting, honest about difficulty, never condescending
- Dominant devices: mini-narratives, normalization of struggle, constraint-as-feature reframe
- Pace: 7/10

## Section 4: Sentence & Structure Rules

**Rhythm pattern:** Alternate 1 longer sentence (15–22 words) with 1–2 shorter sentences (≤12 words). End paragraphs on the shorter sentence.
- *Example:* "The prototype took 6 weeks and cost $80K in contractor time. By the time it launched, the executive had moved on. That's not a development problem—it's a decision-latency problem."

**Opening sentence rule:** Lead with subject-verb. No dependent clause openers in body paragraphs. Hooks may open with a short declarative contradiction.
- *Example:* "Non-technical founders ship faster. Here's why that's not a paradox."

**Paragraph transitions:** Bridge sections with a single-sentence question or a contrast pivot. Never use "Furthermore," "Moreover," or "In conclusion."
- *Example:* "So what does production-ready actually mean for an internal dashboard?"

**Single-sentence paragraphs:** Use sparingly (1–2 per piece) for maximum emphasis. Never two in a row.

---

### Structure Templates

**Template A: Constraint → Mechanism → Reframe**
Best for: thought leadership, analytical content, Marcus persona
```
Hook (name the constraint the reader is living)
→ Diagnosis (why the constraint exists—mechanism, not symptom)
→ Proof (1–2 concrete examples or data points)
→ Reframe (what changes when you see it differently)
→ Single action or principle to carry forward
```

**Template B: Contrarian Inversion**
Best for: tactical content, social posts, Harry/Elena personas
```
Hook (everyone does X; the data shows Y)
→ Why the conventional approach fails (specific mechanism)
→ The inversion (what the top performers do instead)
→ Application (what this means for your context, specifically)
```

**Template C: Validation → Framework → Permission**
Best for: non-technical founder content, Sabrine/Arjun personas
```
Hook (name the fear or struggle directly)
→ Normalize (this is structural, not personal failure)
→ Framework (here's how to think about it)
→ Permission close (you don't need X to start; you need Y)
```

---

### Hook Formulas

- "Everyone thinks [conventional belief]. The [data/mechanism] shows [inversion]."
- "[Specific outcome] in [timeframe]. Here's what actually changed."
- "The [constraint] isn't [assumed cause]. It's [real cause]."
- "[Number] of [audience] [do X]. The ones who [outcome] do [Y] instead."

### Closing Formulas

- "Stop [conventional approach]. Start asking: [reframed question]."
- "[Constraint] is real. So is [what becomes possible when you remove it]."
- "The question isn't [wrong question]. It's [right question]."

## Section 5: Lexicon

**FAVOR**
- `constraint` — names the real problem, not the symptom — 2–3x per piece
- `validate` — testing hypotheses, not building features — 1–2x per piece
- `compress` — time/cost reduction without "faster" hype — 1–2x per piece
- `mechanism` — explains *why*, not just *what* — 1x per piece
- `threshold` — precise boundary vs. vague "enough" — 1x per piece
- `handoff` — code ownership transition, signals exit strategy — 1x per piece
- `scaffold` — what AI generates; implies structure, not finished product — 1–2x per piece
- `surface` — making implicit constraints visible — 1x per piece
- `traction` — earned proof, not projected growth — 1x per piece
- `sandbox` — internal/experimental context vs. production — 1x per piece
- `throughput` — output per unit of time/cost, for agency/enterprise contexts — 1x per piece

**AVOID**
- `production-ready` (unqualified) → say instead: "production-ready for [specific use case and scale]"
- `enterprise-grade` → say instead: "SOC 2 Type II certified, with SSO and audit logs"
- `empower` → say instead: "enable [specific person] to [specific action]"
- `innovative` → say instead: describe the specific capability that's new
- `seamless` → say instead: describe the actual integration step
- `leverage` (as verb) → say instead: "use," "apply," "build on"
- `disruptive` → say instead: describe what changes and for whom
- `vibe coding` (in enterprise/analytical content) → say instead: "AI-assisted full-stack development"
- `game-changer` → say instead: quantify the change
- `best-in-class` → say instead: cite the specific benchmark or certification

**DOMAIN TERMS**
- `70% problem` = the gap between AI-generated scaffold and production-complete application — use when addressing technical audiences; unpack for non-technical personas
- `credit` = one AI interaction unit regardless of complexity — always pair with cost context when first mentioned
- `full-stack generation` = frontend + backend + database + auth + payments from a single prompt — use precisely; don't conflate with frontend-only tools
- `vibe coding` = AI-native app generation from natural language; category term — reserve for community/consumer contexts; avoid in enterprise materials
- `scaffold` = the generated code structure before customization — use to set accurate expectations; never imply it's finished
- `RLS` (Row Level Security) = Supabase database access control — define on first use for non-technical audiences
- `eject` = export generated code to GitHub for IDE-based development — use when addressing developer personas

## Section 6: Analogy & Empathy Patterns

### Analogy Rules

**Approved source domains:**
- **Infrastructure/plumbing** — constraint removal, throughput, bottlenecks (resonates with all personas' operational reality)
- **Construction/scaffolding** — what AI generates vs. what gets built on top (accurate to the 70% problem)
- **Financial/unit economics** — cost compression, runway, ROI (Marcus, Harry, Arjun all think in these terms)
- **Organizational systems** — decision latency, approval cycles, team capacity (Marcus and Harry's daily reality)

**Banned patterns:**
- Sustained metaphors extended across an entire piece — use once, release, return to plain language
- Mystical/journey analogies ("building is a voyage") — no mechanism, no action
- Sports/war conquest framing ("dominate the market") — alienates non-competitive personas
- "CEO of X" constructions — vague; implies identity without specifying behavior

**Analogy templates:**
- "Building [X] without Lovable is like [system with choke point]. You're not slow because of [assumed cause]—you're slow because [actual bottleneck]. Remove that, and [outcome]."
- "What seems like [limitation] is actually [advantage in specific context] because [mechanism]. Just as [familiar system], [Lovable context] works the same way."

---

### Empathy Patterns

**Standard approach:** Name the specific operational constraint before presenting any capability. Never lead with the product. The reader must feel understood before they'll consider a solution.

**Credibility signaling:** Demonstrate narrow, deep knowledge of the reader's specific situation. Never claim broad expertise. "Non-technical founders who've burned $35K on a developer quote" is more credible than "founders everywhere."

**Empathy line templates:**

1. "You've probably gotten a developer quote that made you reconsider the whole idea." — *Sabrine, Arjun*
2. "The prototype was ready three weeks after the executive moved on to the next priority." — *Marcus*
3. "Your margins look fine until a debugging loop eats 40 credits on a fix that didn't work." — *Harry*
4. "The generated code works. You just can't explain to your CTO why it works." — *Elena*
5. "Waiting for IT approval isn't bureaucratic failure. It's the system working exactly as designed—for a different era." — *Marcus*
6. "Hiring a developer in Lagos costs $40K annually. That's not a talent problem. That's a capital structure problem." — *Arjun*
7. "The 30% gap isn't a Lovable limitation. It's where your domain expertise lives." — *Elena, Harry*

## Section 7: Anti-Patterns & Drift Checklist

**NEVER DO THESE**

1. **Unqualified "production-ready" claims** — Triggers: Sabrine's trust killer #1, Elena's trust killer #4 — Do instead: specify use case, scale, and what review is still required
2. **Credit system described without cost context** — Triggers: Harry's trust killer #1, Arjun's trust killer #2 — Do instead: pair every credit mention with a concrete cost example or range
3. **"Replace developers" framing** — Triggers: Marcus's trust killer #3, Elena's trust killer #3 — Do instead: "eliminate boilerplate so developers focus on architecture"
4. **Generic success stories without specifics** — Triggers: Arjun's trust killer #1, Sabrine's trust builder #1 — Do instead: name the founder, the ARR, the timeline, the starting condition
5. **Hedged language in framework sections** — Triggers: all personas' trust in directness — Do instead: state the mechanism; use "may" only for genuine uncertainty, not politeness
6. **Motivational language without mechanism** — Triggers: all personas' skepticism of inspiration-without-system — Do instead: "Confidence comes from shipping. Lovable compresses the shipping cycle."
7. **Treating all personas as non-technical** — Triggers: Elena's trust killer #4 — Do instead: match vocabulary to persona; Elena gets TypeScript specifics, Sabrine gets outcome language
8. **Vague security positioning** — Triggers: Marcus's trust killer #1, Elena's trust killer #3 — Do instead: cite SOC 2 Type II, specific CVE remediation, third-party audit names

## Section 8: Worked Examples

### A. Register Demonstrations

---

**TACTICAL REGISTER** — Harry (Agency Owner) context

The 70% problem isn't a Lovable limitation. It's a business model decision.

Your clients pay for working software. Lovable generates the scaffold—authentication, database schema, Stripe integration, the 40 files that used to eat your first week. That's the 70%. The remaining 30% is where your agency's expertise lives: the edge cases, the client-specific logic, the production hardening. That's also where your margin lives.

The mistake is treating the 70% as incomplete. It's not. It's the part that used to cost you $15K in developer time. Now it costs $200 in credits. The 30% gap is your value-add, not your liability.

*Demonstrates: Tactical register sentence range (9–14 words avg), FAVOR terms "scaffold," "constraint," "threshold" implied, Section 6 empathy pattern (names the fear before reframing), Section 4 contrarian inversion template, closing formula.*

---

**ANALYTICAL REGISTER** — Marcus (Enterprise Innovation Lead) context

The 6-week prototyping cycle isn't a development problem. It's a decision-latency problem—and the distinction matters for how you solve it.

Development cycles are slow because enterprise governance treats every internal tool with the same rigor as a customer-facing product. That's rational risk management for a $2M platform. It's organizational debt for a $10K internal dashboard. The cost isn't the 6 weeks; it's the executive attention that expires during them.

Lovable addresses this by creating a legitimate separation between sandbox experimentation and production deployment. Internal tools and proof-of-concept initiatives operate under lighter governance—not because security matters less, but because the risk profile is genuinely different. The platform's SOC 2 Type II certification and GitHub code export give IT and Compliance the controls they need without requiring full architecture review before a hypothesis is tested.

McKinsey compressed internal tool development from 4–6 months to hours. That's not a speed claim. It's a decision-cycle claim: executives evaluate working prototypes while market windows are still open.

*Demonstrates: Analytical register sentence range (15–22 words avg), Section 4 Constraint → Mechanism → Reframe template, FAVOR terms "compress," "sandbox," "threshold," AVOID term "enterprise-grade" replaced with specific certifications, Section 6 empathy pattern #5.*

---

**EMPATHY REGISTER** — Sabrine (Non-Technical Founder) context

The developer quote wasn't the problem. It was the moment you realized the problem.

$35,000 for an MVP. Or 20% equity for a technical co-founder. Or six months learning to code while your runway depletes. None of those are real options when you have $15,000 in savings and a business hypothesis you've been refining for eight months.

Here's what that quote actually told you: the constraint isn't your idea. It's the cost structure of traditional development. Lovable changes that cost structure—not by making development easier, but by making the first 70% of it invisible. Authentication, database schema, payment flows: generated in hours, not weeks.

Sabrine Matos built Plinq to $456K ARR without a technical co-founder. She didn't wait until she could afford a developer. She validated demand first, then hired. That sequence—validate, then build—is only possible when the cost of validation drops from $35K to $500.

*Demonstrates: Empathy register sentence range (11–16 words avg), Section 6 empathy line template #1, Section 4 Validation → Framework → Permission template, named case study with specific ARR, FAVOR term "validate," no AVOID terms, constraint named before product mentioned.*

### B. Before/After Pairs

---

**Before/After #1: The "Production-Ready" Claim**

BEFORE (generic/industry-typical):
> Lovable generates production-ready full-stack applications from natural language. Our AI builds complete apps with authentication, databases, and payments—ready to deploy in hours, not months. Enterprise-grade security included.

AFTER (Lovable voice):
> Lovable generates the scaffold: React frontend, Supabase backend, authentication, payments. For internal tools and MVP validation, that's production-ready. For customer-facing applications at scale, the generated code is the starting point—clean, exportable, and yours to extend. The 30% gap is where your domain expertise goes.

*What changed: Unqualified "production-ready" replaced with use-case-specific definition (Section 7, Anti-pattern #1). "Enterprise-grade" replaced with specific capabilities (Section 5 AVOID). Constraint named honestly (Section 6 empathy approach). Mechanism explained, not claimed.*

---

**Before/After #2: The Credit System Explanation**

BEFORE (generic/industry-typical):
> Lovable uses a simple credit system. Each AI interaction costs one credit. Pro plan includes 100 monthly credits plus 5 daily credits for $25/month. Credits never expire.

AFTER (Lovable voice):
> One credit, one AI interaction—regardless of complexity. A Pro plan gives you 100 monthly credits plus 5 daily. For context: a typical 5-screen internal dashboard with authentication and a database runs 15–25 credits from blank canvas to working prototype. Debugging loops cost credits too. If the AI suggests a fix that doesn't work, that's a credit spent. We're working on cost-preview before execution. Until then, scope your projects in Chat Mode before switching to Agent Mode for generation.

*What changed: Abstract pricing replaced with concrete cost example (Section 7, Anti-pattern #2). Debugging loop cost acknowledged honestly (Section 6 credibility signaling). Actionable guidance added (Section 4 structure). No AVOID terms.*

### C. Same Topic, Two-Persona Variation

**Topic: The 70% problem**

---

**Version 1 — Elena (Senior Developer)**

Lovable generates clean React/TypeScript with Supabase integration. What it doesn't generate: complex state machines, legacy API integrations, mobile compilation, or the architectural decisions that make a codebase maintainable at 100K lines.

That's the 70%. The remaining 30% is where you work.

Export to GitHub after generation. The code follows standard patterns—you'll recognize it. Customize in VS Code or Cursor. The scaffold eliminates the 2–3 weeks of setup that currently precedes every project without touching the parts that require actual engineering judgment. Your expertise doesn't shrink. The boilerplate does.

*Persona-responsive levers: TypeScript/Supabase specifics named, IDE references (VS Code, Cursor), "scaffold" framing, no hand-holding on basic concepts, constraint stated precisely.*

---

**Version 2 — Sabrine (Non-Technical Founder)**

Lovable builds 70% of your application automatically—the authentication, the database, the payment flows, the screens. That's the part that used to cost $35K and six weeks.

The remaining 30% is the part that requires knowing your business: the specific workflow your customers need, the edge cases in your industry, the logic that makes your product different. That's not a gap in the tool. That's where you contribute.

For an MVP validating market demand, 70% is enough. Sabrine Matos reached $456K ARR before hiring her first developer. She validated demand with the 70%. Then she hired to close the gap.

*Persona-responsive levers: No technical jargon, cost anchored to familiar $35K quote, 30% reframed as founder contribution not limitation, named case study, permission-granting close.*

**What stays constant (voice bedrock):** Honest constraint naming, specific numbers, mechanism explained before product claimed, no motivational language, constraint-as-feature reframe.

**What shifts (persona-responsive levers):** Technical vocabulary depth, analogies drawn from daily reality, case study selection, level of implementation detail.
